AI Answers. But Who’s Thinking?

Artificial intelligence through the eyes of a process management expert

Kuritár Viktória

When AI first entered my life, everyone seemed to be saying, “Oh my God, this is amazing!” Ask it anything and it answers almost instantly. All you have to do is ask.

And to me, that is exactly the point. Asking questions.

Of course, the predictions soon followed: AI would eventually take everyone's jobs. Whether that happens is for the future to decide. Right now, I'm much more interested in how astonishingly quickly we seem willing to give up thinking for ourselves.

Anyone who was already happy with the first obvious answer has it even easier now. Type in two words and out come the world-changing ideas. It takes even less effort than the days when people might, on a good day, read the first three Google results.

But just because we get an answer quickly, does that mean we actually understand it?

I think AI is a fantastic tool. It helps me organize ideas, find new perspectives and, perhaps most importantly, save time. Not so I can think less, but so I can go deeper into the work I'm putting my name to.

Process management isn't about drawing boxes

I often hear: “Viki, AI is going to take your job. It'll draw your process maps for you.”

Well, let it! Just because a process map looks beautiful and logical doesn't mean it reflects how things actually work.

For me, one of the most important tools in process management is intuition. When I ask someone a question, I don't just listen to what they say. I pay attention to how they say it: their tone, body language, hesitation. Sometimes eye contact held a fraction too long, a raised eyebrow or a sigh reveals more than a carefully constructed answer.

Those signals help me sense which direction to take next. I can be wrong, of course. And when I am, I ask more questions.

Because a human answer is never just information. It carries someone's upbringing, values, experience, biases, the traffic jam that morning or the book they read the night before. And who knows what else?

That's exactly what makes us interesting. The same person may answer the same question differently at two different moments.

A tennis player does more than hit the ball

I love tennis. A good player doesn't just practice shots. They study their own matches and their opponents', learn, prepare and practice again and again.

Then they step onto the court. And something happens that no amount of practice can fully prepare them for.

I remember Serena Williams in the 2001 Indian Wells final. Much of the crowd booed her and even cheered her mistakes. She lost the first set to Kim Clijsters, but fought back to win the match. She later recalled crying for hours in the locker room after her victory.

How much mental strength must it have taken to keep playing, let alone win, in those circumstances?

Behind every practiced movement was a human being. Someone making decisions in the moment, adapting, somehow finding a way through.

That, to me, is the difference between having knowledge and truly knowing how to use it.

What we put our name to says something about us

There's something else on my mind: respect.

If I hand someone a piece of AI-generated work that I've barely reviewed myself, what am I telling them? That my time matters more than theirs?

And what does that say about me?

I believe the quality of our work reflects more than our professional skills. It says something about how we think, what we value and how much we respect other people.

Our individual perspective is what sets us apart. Why would we choose to give that up?

It's not AI I'm afraid of

I think anyone who doesn't want to be left behind needs to learn to use AI. And now is the time, while we're still shaping our habits around it.

But let's not just learn how to get fast answers. Let's learn to challenge them.

Ask again! Check the facts! Argue with it! Not until it finally tells us what we want to hear — a carefully worded prompt can make that all too easy — but until we have an answer we genuinely understand and can reconcile with our own knowledge, experience and values.

And perhaps most importantly, let's be willing to say: I don't agree.

What worries me isn't that AI will become capable of more and more.

It's that we may want to use less and less of our own abilities.

AI is a wonderful tool. But our independent thinking, our intuition and our individuality are worth far more than a few convenient answers.

Because the answer may come from the machine. But the thinking should be ours.
