I Just Draw Boxes Next to Each Other…

The knowledge, practice and thousands of boxes behind the illusion of effortlessness

Kuritár Viktória

There is a pattern I keep encountering as a process improvement professional. Wherever I go, people tend to welcome me with great enthusiasm.

“How wonderful that you're here! Finally, someone will make our processes clear!”

At first, everyone is happy. After all, who wouldn't want transparent, logical, well-functioning processes?

Then the actual work begins. And so do the questions.

Why do we do it this way? What happens next? Who decides? What if things go differently?

Somewhere around here, those few little boxes start to look less simple.

From four boxes to forty-two

Once, I sat down with a colleague to document their part in a process.

“Oh, this will only take fifteen minutes!” they said confidently.

They described their work in four steps.

There was nothing wrong with that. For a high-level overview—what we might call L1—it could have been perfectly sufficient.

But we needed documentation detailed enough to support future improvements. That meant going deeper, at least to an L3 level of detail.

So I started asking questions. And more questions. And then some more.

Almost an hour and a half later, those four steps had become a forty-two-step process map.

And my colleague started to cry.

Not because my questions had exhausted them.

But because, for the first time, they could see just how complex their work really was.

They knew how much they did every day, yet they had never managed to communicate it in a way their managers could truly understand.

Now it was there, in front of them: visible, structured and connected in a single process.

I will never forget that moment.

If all I had done was draw boxes, we would have stopped at four.

Finding the other thirty-eight required knowing how to ask.

Mind-reading isn't one of my skills

My job often sounds simple: map out the process that exists in someone else's head.

There is one small problem. I cannot read minds.

So I turn to the good old art of asking questions.

When I ask someone to describe exactly what they do, I often encounter one of two situations.

Sometimes they fear for their job. They worry that my questions hide an ulterior motive. Perhaps I am checking up on them. Perhaps I want to eliminate their tasks.

At other times, they want to explain—but struggle to find the words.

They understand their work and perform it every day, often automatically. But breaking it into steps, naming decisions and explaining connections can suddenly be difficult.

Each situation requires a different approach. Both demand far more than knowing how to use a diagramming tool.

Sometimes your ego needs to stay outside the door

As a process improvement professional, I cannot always choose who I work with.

I have even encountered someone who simply laughed at me.

“Oh, how sweet that you want to draw this!”

Yes. That is what starting at a disadvantage looks like.

My ego could take over. I could try to prove that I know what I'm doing and why it matters.

But that isn't why I sat down with them. I am there to understand and document a process.

Sometimes I need to put my own ego aside and let the other person know that they are the expert in their work. Their knowledge is what I need.

They do not have to draw, structure or document the process. I will do that.

They simply need to tell me how they work.

At the same time, I cannot lose control of the conversation. The goal is not to enjoy a coffee and then go our separate ways.

The goal is to leave with a process we understand together and have documented.

That takes humility. And sometimes a considerable amount of diplomacy.

Flexibility is not optional

If I counted all the times I have had to adapt to colleagues during process discovery, I would probably surprise myself.

Trust doesn't begin by announcing when I'm available and expecting everyone else to adjust.

Sometimes we meet when it suits the other person—when they have time and feel ready to share knowledge they may never have had to put into words so precisely.

And during the conversation I must adapt to how they think.

Some people describe their steps in perfect sequence.

Others are already at the end of the process by their third sentence, before jumping back to the beginning and remembering something from the middle.

And some are mentally writing their shopping list while describing the process.

Meanwhile, I ask, listen, structure and draw. In real time.

By the end, what lived in someone else's head needs to appear in a clear, logical and understandable form.

From the outside, that can look surprisingly easy. Behind it are countless hours of practice.

Common sense alone isn't enough

I often hear that drawing process maps is just common sense.

“So that's how you draw them? I could do that!”

My usual answer is: “Great! Let's try it.”

Let's reverse roles. I'll suggest a topic; you interview me and map the process.

In my experience, it quickly becomes clear that it isn't quite so obvious.

Because what happens when the answer is not yes or no?

What if it's: “Well… yes and no.”

Or: “It depends.”

Or: “Usually we do it this way, except when we don't.”

This is where things become interesting.

It's not enough to know how to draw a decision diamond.

You need to know which question reveals what the decision actually depends on.

And we're still not finished

Let's say we've discovered the process.

Every step is in place, decisions are clear and connections are visible.

The process map is complete. Are we done?

Not quite.

Process documentation often means much more than that.

Steps need identifiers. We must define responsibilities, connected systems and, where relevant, time requirements and performance indicators. We may also need a RACI matrix, detailed descriptions and supporting documentation.

And we haven't even mentioned automation.

If we want to automate a process, even L3 detail may not be enough.

“User enters the data” is no longer a sufficient instruction.

Which data? In which system? Which fields? Under what conditions? What if something is missing, incorrect, or the system doesn't respond?

And so it continues.

I won't wear out the reader with every last detail.

Let's just say there are quite a few more “boxes” to draw.

The work behind the effortlessness

Tennis is one of my favourite sports.

What has always fascinated me about Roger Federer is the effortless elegance of his movement on court.

Sometimes it seemed as though hitting the ball required no effort at all.

One movement. One serve. One moment.

But how much practice did it take to make it look so easy?

How many thousands of shots, mistimed movements and hours of focused practice? How much discipline and perseverance?

Perhaps not even he could count it precisely.

I am not comparing process improvement to professional tennis, nor claiming to be the Roger Federer of my field.

The comparison is about something else.

When someone practises something long enough, even a difficult skill can look effortless to an observer.

We see the result of a professional's work.

What we rarely see are the years of learning, trial and error, adjustment and persistence behind it.

There is a curious paradox here.

The more successfully we simplify something, the less visible its original complexity becomes.

Yet before I can present something simply and logically, I have to understand it myself.

Because I cannot map something logically if I do not understand it.

So yes. I draw boxes.

Sometimes thousands of them.

But learning to draw the boxes wasn't what took years.

It was learning to ask, listen and understand people and their work. To find connections and turn complexity into a clear, usable process.

To make visible to others what once existed only in someone's head.

And when I do that well, the result may look perfectly simple.

Perhaps that is the hardest part of all.

L1 and L3 refer to levels of process detail: a high-level overview versus a more detailed process map. RACI is a responsibility-assignment matrix.
