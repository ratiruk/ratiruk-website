The Glorious 1,300 Metres

When we think everything is perfect. Except it doesn’t work.

Kuritár Viktória

I first heard the story of the Swedish warship Vasa during a project management course. It stayed with me, and every now and then I still find myself thinking about it.

Not only because it is remarkable how many things can go wrong in a single project, but because almost four centuries later, the situations it reveals feel surprisingly familiar.

But let’s start at the beginning.

In 1626, work began on a warship commissioned by King Gustav II Adolf of Sweden, one that was meant to leave the rest of Europe speechless.

In a way, it succeeded. Just not quite as planned.

Vasa was nearly 70 metres long, armed with 64 bronze cannons and covered in elaborate carvings and gilded figures. A floating palace designed to intimidate the enemy on sight.

It had everything: money, ambition, splendour, formidable firepower.

The ability to stay afloat, however, was somewhat underrepresented.

When things start going sideways

Building Vasa was anything but straightforward. The master shipwright, Henrik Hybertsson, became ill and died during construction. His assistant, Hein Jakobsson, took over.

They did not work from the kind of detailed plans we expect today. Much of the knowledge lay in the shipwrights’ experience and in decisions made along the way.

So the master was gone, and the others were left with a half-built, remarkably ambitious undertaking.

Let’s just say the knowledge handover would not meet today’s expectations.

That is the first lesson I take from the story: documentation does not exist so we have something to upload to a shared folder. It exists so decisions, reasoning and connections do not live in just one person’s head.

Different groups of craftspeople also worked on the ship. Surviving measuring tools show that both Swedish and Dutch units were used. Whether that played a role in the disaster is uncertain.

It reminds me of one of my own rules:

Let’s synchronise our watches!

We can have as many people as we like working towards the same goal. Without regular communication, everyone may be making excellent progress.

Just in different directions.

The test that worked a little too well

Vasa was finally completed. For the stability test, thirty sailors ran from one side of the deck to the other to see how far the ship would lean.

They tested it.

They got worried.

And stopped the test, fearing the ship could capsize right there in the harbour.

They did not stop the ship. They stopped the test.

On 10 August 1628, Vasa ceremoniously set sail from Stockholm harbour.

A gust of wind arrived. The ship heeled over, water poured through the open gunports, and within minutes it sank.

Distance travelled: roughly 1,300 metres.

Two years of construction, enormous expense, spectacular ambition. And it barely made it out of the harbour.

People lost their lives in the disaster, so the tragedy itself is no laughing matter. But the decisions and oversights that led to it still give us plenty to think about.

Making decisions is easy. Living with the consequences is another matter.

To me, this story is also about responsibility.

We all make decisions. And sometimes we make the wrong ones.

What matters is what we do when we discover that something does not work the way we expected.

Do we cling to the original idea because we have already invested too much in it? Or are we willing to change course and accept the consequences?

This is not just a leadership responsibility. Those doing the work must also ask questions, raise concerns and look beyond ticking off their own tasks.

And leaders must create an environment where those concerns are genuinely heard.

Not talking about a problem does not make it disappear.

Physics, for one, was clearly unimpressed by the Swedish king’s ambitions.

And that brings me to Kaizen

For me, Kaizen — continuous improvement — is much more than a methodology.

It is a mindset.

It means recognising that something that works well today may not work quite so well tomorrow.

Circumstances change. Expectations, people and opportunities change. That is why we need to revisit not only our processes, but also our own thinking and the decisions we have made.

Not because everything we have done so far is wrong.

But because a decision that was right once is not necessarily right forever.

And that does not always call for a major transformation. Sometimes honest feedback, a good question or one small adjustment is enough.

Or the realisation that it is time to synchronise our watches again.

Progress does not mean we can never be satisfied. It means satisfaction should never mark the end of improvement.

A ship that eventually became famous after all

Vasa was raised from the seabed 333 years later, in 1961. Today it is one of Stockholm’s best-known museum attractions.

As a warship, a complete failure. As a museum exhibit, a worldwide success.

It seems they eventually found the job it was truly suited for.

What I take from the story is that it is not enough to design, build and deliver something.

We have to talk. Document. Align. Ask. Listen to the answers. And change course when needed.

Because the most important question is not necessarily whether something has been completed.

It is this:

Looks great. But will it float?

And if it floated yesterday, will it still float tomorrow?
